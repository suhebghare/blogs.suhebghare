SLOs, SLIs, and Error Budgets That Actually Work in Production

👁️980reads
👍412likes
👎22dislikes

Most teams I've worked with either don't have SLOs, or have SLOs that nobody looks at. They're set once during a planning meeting, written in a Confluence page, and never connected to actual alerting or decision-making. That's not SRE — that's documentation theater.

This post covers how to implement SLOs that actually drive engineering decisions, with real Prometheus recording rules, burn rate alerts, and an error budget policy that your team will actually follow.

SLI, SLO, SLA — Cut Through the Confusion

These three terms get mixed up constantly. Here's the simplest way to think about them:

Term What It Is Who Owns It Example
SLI (Service Level Indicator) A measurement — a ratio of good events to total events Engineering 99.2% of requests returned 2xx in the last 30 days
SLO (Service Level Objective) A target — the threshold your SLI should meet Engineering + Product 99.9% of requests should return 2xx over 30 days
SLA (Service Level Agreement) A contract — the SLO with financial consequences Business + Legal If availability drops below 99.5%, customer gets 10% credit
The Golden Rule

Your SLO should always be stricter than your SLA. If your SLA promises 99.5%, your internal SLO should be 99.9%. The gap between them is your safety margin. If you alert on SLA breach, you're already in contract violation by the time you respond.

Choosing the Right SLIs

Not everything needs an SLO. Focus on what users actually experience. For most services, you need exactly two SLIs:

Availability
Ratio of successful requests to total requests. "Did the user get a response?" This catches outages, 5xx errors, and timeouts.
Latency
Ratio of requests faster than a threshold to total requests. "Was the response fast enough?" Use P99 for critical paths, P95 for others.

For specific service types, add a third:

  • Data pipelines: Freshness — "Is the data less than X minutes old?"
  • Batch jobs: Throughput — "Did we process X records per hour?"
  • Storage systems: Durability — "What percentage of writes were persisted?"

Setting the Right SLO Target

This is where most teams go wrong. They pick 99.99% because it sounds good. Then they spend all their engineering time chasing reliability instead of building features. Or they pick 99% because it's easy, and users complain constantly.

The Real Cost of Each Nine

SLO Allowed Downtime/month Error Budget (30 days) What It Means in Practice
99% 7h 18m 432,000 bad requests per 43.2M Can deploy aggressively, tolerate some failures
99.9% 43m 50s 43,200 bad requests per 43.2M Need good deploys, basic alerting, reasonable on-call
99.95% 21m 55s 21,600 bad requests per 43.2M Need canary deploys, fast rollback, solid monitoring
99.99% 4m 23s 4,320 bad requests per 43.2M Need redundancy, auto-failover, minimal human intervention
Real Example: 99.9% vs 99.95% — The Cost

We ran our checkout API at 99.95% for 6 months. The engineering cost to maintain it was roughly 1.5 FTE — dedicated to reliability work, on-call improvements, and incident response. When we relaxed to 99.9%, we freed up that engineer to build features. The extra 21 minutes of allowed downtime per month translated to ~$40K/year in recovered engineering capacity. The business impact of the extra downtime? Negligible — our actual availability was 99.93% either way.

How to Pick Your Target

  1. Measure your current SLI for 30 days before setting an SLO. If you're currently at 99.7%, don't set an SLO of 99.99%.
  2. Ask product: "What's the user impact of X minutes of downtime per month?" Not "what availability do you want?" (they'll always say 100%).
  3. Start loose, tighten later. It's much easier to tighten an SLO than to relax one. Start at 99.9% and see if users complain.
  4. Different SLOs for different endpoints. Your checkout API needs 99.95%. Your admin dashboard can live with 99.5%.

Error Budget: The Math

The error budget is simply: 1 - SLO. If your SLO is 99.9%, your error budget is 0.1% of total requests over the SLO window.

For a service handling 1,000 requests per minute over a 30-day window:

Total requests in 30 days: 1,000 × 60 × 24 × 30 = 43,200,000

Error budget (99.9% SLO): 43,200,000 × 0.001 = 43,200 failed requests

That's ~1 failed request per minute sustained over the entire month, or a 100% outage for ~43 minutes.

Prometheus Recording Rules for SLIs

Recording rules pre-compute your SLIs so dashboards and alerts are fast. Here's what I use for every service:

groups:
- name: sli-recording-rules
  interval: 30s
  rules:
  # Availability SLI — ratio of non-5xx requests
  - record: sli:availability:ratio_rate5m
    expr: |
      sum(rate(http_requests_total{status!~"5.."}[5m])) by (service)
      /
      sum(rate(http_requests_total[5m])) by (service)

  # Availability SLI — 30-day rolling window
  - record: sli:availability:ratio_rate30d
    expr: |
      sum(increase(http_requests_total{status!~"5.."}[30d])) by (service)
      /
      sum(increase(http_requests_total[30d])) by (service)

  # Latency SLI — ratio of requests under 300ms
  - record: sli:latency:ratio_rate5m
    expr: |
      sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{le="0.3"}[5m])) by (service)
      /
      sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_count[5m])) by (service)

  # Error budget remaining (availability)
  - record: sli:error_budget:remaining
    expr: |
      1 - (
        (1 - sli:availability:ratio_rate30d)
        /
        (1 - 0.999)
      )

The sli:error_budget:remaining metric gives you a value between 0 and 1. At 1.0, you haven't used any budget. At 0, you've exhausted it. Below 0, you're in violation.

Burn Rate Alerting: Stop Alerting on Thresholds

Traditional alerting fires when availability drops below 99.9% over 5 minutes. This is noisy and misses slow burns. Burn rate alerting asks a better question: "At the current rate of errors, when will we exhaust our error budget?"

A burn rate of 1x means you'll exactly exhaust your budget by the end of the window. A burn rate of 14.4x means you'll exhaust it in 1/14.4th of the window — about 2 days for a 30-day window.

Burn Rate 1x → Budget exhausted in 30 days → No alert
Burn Rate 6x → Budget exhausted in 5 days → Ticket (slow burn)
Burn Rate 14.4x → Budget exhausted in 2 days → Page (fast burn)
Burn Rate 36x → Budget exhausted in 20 hours → Critical page

Multi-Window Burn Rate Alerts

Google's SRE book recommends multi-window alerts to reduce false positives. You check both a long window (has enough budget been burned?) and a short window (is it still burning?):

groups:
- name: slo-burn-rate-alerts
  rules:
  # Critical: 2% of 30-day budget burned in 1 hour
  # (still burning in last 5 minutes)
  - alert: SLOBurnRateCritical
    expr: |
      (
        (1 - sli:availability:ratio_rate1h) / (1 - 0.999)
      ) > 14.4
      and
      (
        (1 - sli:availability:ratio_rate5m) / (1 - 0.999)
      ) > 14.4
    for: 2m
    labels:
      severity: critical
    annotations:
      summary: "High error burn rate on {{ $labels.service }}"
      description: "Service {{ $labels.service }} is burning error budget 14.4x faster than normal. Budget will be exhausted in ~2 days at this rate."

  # Warning: 5% of 30-day budget burned in 6 hours
  - alert: SLOBurnRateWarning
    expr: |
      (
        (1 - sli:availability:ratio_rate6h) / (1 - 0.999)
      ) > 6
      and
      (
        (1 - sli:availability:ratio_rate30m) / (1 - 0.999)
      ) > 6
    for: 5m
    labels:
      severity: warning
    annotations:
      summary: "Elevated error burn rate on {{ $labels.service }}"
      description: "Service {{ $labels.service }} is burning error budget 6x faster than normal."
Impact of Switching to Burn Rate Alerts

After migrating from threshold-based to burn rate alerts across 23 services: alert volume dropped by 68%. Pages dropped by 41%. But we caught 100% of the incidents that actually impacted users — we just stopped waking people up for blips that self-resolved in 30 seconds.

The Error Budget Policy

An error budget is useless without a policy that defines what happens when it's consumed. This is the document that gives SRE teeth. Without it, product will always push for more features regardless of reliability.

Our Error Budget Policy

Budget Remaining Status Actions
>50% Healthy Normal development velocity. Deploy freely. Take on tech debt if needed.
25-50% Caution Review recent incidents. Prioritize one reliability task per sprint. Canary all deploys.
10-25% At Risk Freeze non-critical deploys. Dedicate 50% of sprint to reliability. Mandatory rollback plan for every deploy.
<10% Exhausted Feature freeze. All engineering effort on reliability. Only emergency fixes deployed. Post-mortem required for any further budget consumption.
Getting Buy-In for the Policy

The error budget policy only works if engineering leadership and product management both sign off. Present it as a trade-off framework, not a punishment. "When we're reliable, we ship fast. When we're not, we fix reliability first." Frame it in terms of user trust and revenue, not uptime percentages.

Building the SLO Dashboard

Every service gets a Grafana dashboard with these panels:

  • Current SLI (30-day rolling) — big number, green/yellow/red
  • Error budget remaining — percentage gauge
  • Error budget burn-down — time series showing budget consumption over the month
  • Burn rate (1h, 6h windows) — shows current burn velocity
  • SLI over time — 5-minute resolution, 30-day view
  • Top error contributors — which endpoints are consuming the most budget

The most important panel is the burn-down chart. It makes error budget tangible. When the team sees the line dropping, they understand the urgency viscerally in a way that "99.87% availability" doesn't convey.

Common Mistakes

  • Setting SLOs without measuring current SLIs first — you'll set unrealistic targets
  • Using the same SLO for all endpoints — your health check doesn't need 99.99%
  • Alerting on SLO breach instead of burn rate — you'll get paged for 30-second blips
  • Not having an error budget policy — SLOs without consequences are just dashboards
  • Counting only 5xx as errors — timeouts, client-visible retries, and slow responses are errors too
  • Using too short a window — 5-minute SLIs are noisy; 30-day rolling windows smooth out blips
  • Not excluding health checks and synthetic monitoring from SLI calculations

Implementing SLOs: A 4-Week Plan

Week 1: Instrument

Add HTTP request counters and latency histograms to all services. Use standard labels: service, method, status, endpoint. Deploy Prometheus recording rules for SLIs.

Week 2: Measure

Let the metrics collect for a week. Build the Grafana dashboard. Identify your current baseline SLIs. Share with the team — "here's where we actually are."

Week 3: Set Targets

Meet with product and engineering leads. Set initial SLOs based on measured baselines (slightly above current performance). Define the error budget policy. Get sign-off.

Week 4: Alert

Deploy burn rate alerts. Run them in parallel with existing alerts for one week. Compare signal quality. Decommission old threshold alerts. Celebrate fewer pages.

Key Takeaways

SLOs are not about achieving perfect reliability. They're about making reliability a conscious, measurable trade-off against feature velocity. The error budget gives you permission to take risks when you're reliable, and forces you to slow down when you're not. That's the entire point. Start measuring today, set conservative targets, and iterate. The hardest part isn't the Prometheus rules — it's getting your organization to actually use the error budget as a decision-making tool.
