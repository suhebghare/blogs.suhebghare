Prometheus Alerting Rules That Don't Page You for Nothing

Last year, our on-call engineers were getting paged 200+ times per week. Most of those alerts were noise — transient CPU spikes, brief latency bumps that resolved before anyone could even look at them, and threshold-based rules that fired on perfectly healthy systems during normal traffic patterns. Engineers started ignoring alerts. That's when the real incidents slipped through.

We spent three months rewriting every alerting rule from scratch. The result: 12 actionable alerts per week, zero missed incidents, and on-call engineers who actually sleep through the night. Here's exactly how we did it.

The Real Cost of Alert Fatigue

Alert fatigue doesn't just burn out engineers — it causes real incidents. When your team gets 30+ alerts per day, the response to every alert becomes "probably nothing." That's how a genuine database failover gets ignored for 23 minutes.

Why Most Alerting Rules Are Wrong

The default approach most teams take is threshold-based alerting: CPU > 80%? Alert. Memory > 90%? Alert. Response time > 500ms? Alert. This approach is fundamentally broken for three reasons:

  • Thresholds ignore context. A node running at 85% CPU during a batch job at 3 AM is not the same as 85% CPU during peak traffic with no headroom.
  • Point-in-time checks miss trends. A disk at 70% that's been stable for months is fine. A disk at 70% that was at 40% yesterday is an emergency.
  • Cause-based alerts create noise. High CPU is a cause. Slow user requests is a symptom. Users don't care about your CPU — they care about latency.

The shift we made was from cause-based alerting to symptom-based alerting. Instead of alerting on "CPU is high," we alert on "users are experiencing errors" or "checkout latency exceeds SLO." The CPU metric still exists — it's in the dashboard for debugging. But it doesn't page anyone at 3 AM.

Multi-Window, Multi-Burn-Rate Alerting

This is the single most impactful change we made. Instead of a simple threshold ("error rate > 1%"), we use burn-rate alerting based on our SLO error budget. The concept comes from Google's SRE book, and it works like this:

If your SLO is 99.9% availability over 30 days, you have an error budget of 0.1% — roughly 43 minutes of downtime per month. A burn rate of 1x means you're consuming that budget at exactly the expected rate. A burn rate of 14.4x means you'll exhaust your entire monthly budget in 2 hours.

We use multiple windows to catch both fast burns (something is very broken right now) and slow burns (something is degraded and will eventually exhaust the budget):

SeverityBurn RateLong WindowShort WindowBudget ConsumedAction
Critical14.4x1h5m2% in 1hPage immediately
Critical6x6h30m5% in 6hPage
Warning3x1d2h10% in 1dTicket
Info1x3d6h10% in 3dReview

The dual-window approach is critical. The long window ensures the problem is real and sustained. The short window ensures the problem is still happening right now (not something that already resolved). Both conditions must be true to fire.

Real PromQL Rules That Work

API Error Rate (Multi-Burn-Rate)

This is our primary SLO-based alert. It fires when the error rate is burning through our error budget too fast:

# Critical: 14.4x burn rate — exhausts monthly budget in 2 hours
- alert: APIHighErrorBurnRate
  expr: |
    (
      sum(rate(http_requests_total{status=~"5..",job="api-server"}[1h]))
      /
      sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="api-server"}[1h]))
    ) > (14.4 * 0.001)
    and
    (
      sum(rate(http_requests_total{status=~"5..",job="api-server"}[5m]))
      /
      sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="api-server"}[5m]))
    ) > (14.4 * 0.001)
  for: 2m
  labels:
    severity: critical
    team: platform
    slo: availability
  annotations:
    summary: "API error rate burning through SLO budget at 14.4x"
    description: "Error rate is {{ $value | humanizePercentage }} over the last hour. At this rate, the monthly error budget will be exhausted in ~2 hours."
    runbook_url: "https://runbooks.internal/api-high-error-rate"
    dashboard: "https://grafana.internal/d/api-overview"
Why the for: 2m clause?

The for clause adds a pending period. The alert must be continuously firing for 2 minutes before it actually triggers. This catches the edge case where a single scrape returns bad data. Don't set this too high though — for critical alerts, 1-2 minutes is the sweet spot.

Latency P99 SLO Breach

We track p99 latency against a 500ms SLO. The histogram_quantile function is your friend here:

- alert: APILatencyP99High
  expr: |
    (
      histogram_quantile(0.99,
        sum(rate(http_request_duration_seconds_bucket{job="api-server"}[5m])) by (le)
      ) > 0.5
    )
    and
    (
      sum(rate(http_requests_total{job="api-server"}[5m])) > 10
    )
  for: 5m
  labels:
    severity: warning
    team: platform
  annotations:
    summary: "API p99 latency exceeds 500ms SLO"
    description: "P99 latency is {{ $value | humanizeDuration }}. SLO target is 500ms."
    runbook_url: "https://runbooks.internal/api-high-latency"

Notice the second condition: sum(rate(...)) > 10. This ensures we only alert when there's meaningful traffic. A single slow request at 4 AM shouldn't page anyone.

Pod OOMKilled Detection

OOMKilled pods are a leading indicator of memory leaks or undersized resource limits. This rule catches pods that are repeatedly OOMKilled:

- alert: PodOOMKilledRepeated
  expr: |
    (
      kube_pod_container_status_last_terminated_reason{reason="OOMKilled"} == 1
    )
    * on(namespace, pod) group_left()
    (
      increase(kube_pod_container_status_restarts_total[1h]) > 2
    )
  for: 0m
  labels:
    severity: warning
    team: "{{ $labels.namespace }}"
  annotations:
    summary: "Pod {{ $labels.namespace }}/{{ $labels.pod }} OOMKilled 3+ times in 1h"
    description: "Container {{ $labels.container }} is being repeatedly OOMKilled. Current memory limit may be too low, or there's a memory leak."
    runbook_url: "https://runbooks.internal/pod-oomkilled"

Disk Filling Up — Predictive Alert

This is one of my favorite rules. Instead of alerting when disk is at 90% (which might be fine for months), we predict when the disk will be full based on the current growth rate:

- alert: DiskWillFillIn4Hours
  expr: |
    (
      node_filesystem_avail_bytes{fstype!~"tmpfs|overlay"}
      /
      node_filesystem_size_bytes{fstype!~"tmpfs|overlay"}
      < 0.20
    )
    and
    (
      predict_linear(
        node_filesystem_avail_bytes{fstype!~"tmpfs|overlay"}[6h], 4 * 3600
      ) < 0
    )
  for: 10m
  labels:
    severity: critical
  annotations:
    summary: "Disk {{ $labels.mountpoint }} on {{ $labels.instance }} will fill within 4 hours"
    description: "Current available space is {{ $value | humanize1024 }}B. Based on the last 6 hours of growth, the disk will be full in approximately 4 hours."
    runbook_url: "https://runbooks.internal/disk-filling"

The predict_linear function uses linear regression over the last 6 hours to project when available space hits zero. We combine it with a current threshold (< 20% free) to avoid false positives on disks that are mostly full but stable.

Node Not Ready

- alert: KubernetesNodeNotReady
  expr: |
    kube_node_status_condition{condition="Ready",status="true"} == 0
  for: 5m
  labels:
    severity: critical
  annotations:
    summary: "Node {{ $labels.node }} is NotReady for 5+ minutes"
    description: "Node has been in NotReady state. This affects all pods scheduled on this node. Check kubelet logs and node conditions."
    runbook_url: "https://runbooks.internal/node-not-ready"
Don't Alert on Single Node Failures in Large Clusters

If you have 50+ nodes, a single node going NotReady is expected and Kubernetes handles it automatically via rescheduling. Consider alerting only when multiple nodes are NotReady simultaneously, or when the NotReady node is running critical workloads without PodDisruptionBudgets.

Alertmanager Routing and Grouping

Writing good alerting rules is only half the battle. If your routing is wrong, the right people don't see the right alerts. Here's our Alertmanager config structure:

route:
  receiver: 'default-slack'
  group_by: ['alertname', 'namespace', 'severity']
  group_wait: 30s
  group_interval: 5m
  repeat_interval: 4h

  routes:
    # Critical alerts → PagerDuty immediately
    - match:
        severity: critical
      receiver: 'pagerduty-critical'
      group_wait: 10s
      repeat_interval: 1h
      continue: false

    # Warning alerts → Slack channel, create ticket
    - match:
        severity: warning
      receiver: 'slack-warnings'
      group_wait: 1m
      repeat_interval: 8h

    # Team-specific routing
    - match_re:
        team: 'payments|checkout'
      receiver: 'slack-payments-team'
      continue: true

    # Inhibit noisy namespaces during deployments
    - match:
        namespace: 'staging'
      receiver: 'slack-staging'
      repeat_interval: 24h

receivers:
  - name: 'pagerduty-critical'
    pagerduty_configs:
      - service_key_file: '/etc/alertmanager/secrets/pagerduty-key'
        description: '{{ .CommonAnnotations.summary }}'
        details:
          runbook: '{{ .CommonAnnotations.runbook_url }}'
          dashboard: '{{ .CommonAnnotations.dashboard }}'

  - name: 'slack-warnings'
    slack_configs:
      - api_url_file: '/etc/alertmanager/secrets/slack-webhook'
        channel: '#alerts-warning'
        title: '{{ .CommonAnnotations.summary }}'
        text: '{{ .CommonAnnotations.description }}'
        actions:
          - type: button
            text: 'Runbook'
            url: '{{ .CommonAnnotations.runbook_url }}'
          - type: button
            text: 'Dashboard'
            url: '{{ .CommonAnnotations.dashboard }}'

Grouping Strategy

Grouping is how Alertmanager batches related alerts into a single notification. Get this wrong and you'll either get one notification per alert (noisy) or all alerts in one blob (useless).

Our grouping strategy:

  • group_by: ['alertname', 'namespace', 'severity'] — Groups the same alert type within the same namespace. So if 5 pods in the payments namespace are OOMKilled, you get one notification listing all 5.
  • group_wait: 30s — Waits 30 seconds to collect related alerts before sending. This prevents getting 5 separate notifications when a cascading failure triggers multiple alerts within seconds.
  • group_interval: 5m — If new alerts join an existing group, wait 5 minutes before sending an update.
  • repeat_interval: 4h — Don't re-send the same alert group more than once every 4 hours. For critical alerts, we use 1h.

Inhibition Rules

Inhibition rules suppress alerts when a higher-level alert is already firing. This is essential for preventing alert storms during cascading failures:

inhibit_rules:
  # If a node is down, suppress all pod alerts on that node
  - source_match:
      alertname: KubernetesNodeNotReady
    target_match_re:
      alertname: 'Pod.*|Container.*'
    equal: ['instance']

  # If the cluster is unhealthy, suppress individual service alerts
  - source_match:
      alertname: KubernetesClusterUnhealthy
    target_match:
      severity: warning

  # Critical suppresses warning for the same alert
  - source_match:
      severity: critical
    target_match:
      severity: warning
    equal: ['alertname', 'namespace']

The last rule is particularly important. When an alert escalates from warning to critical, you don't want both notifications firing. The critical alert inhibits the warning for the same alertname and namespace.

Silence vs. Fix: The Rule We Enforce

We have a strict team policy: every silence must have a corresponding Jira ticket. Silences are not a fix — they're a temporary acknowledgment that you know about the problem and are working on it.

Our silence policy: Maximum silence duration is 7 days. If you need to silence an alert for longer, the alert rule itself needs to be fixed. We review all active silences in our weekly SRE sync.

We also track a metric we call the silence ratio — the percentage of alerts that are silenced at any given time. If this exceeds 20%, it's a signal that our alerting rules need a review pass. A high silence ratio means we're generating alerts we don't actually care about.

Runbook Links: Non-Negotiable

Every single alert must have a runbook_url annotation. No exceptions. When an engineer gets paged at 3 AM, they should not have to figure out what the alert means or what to do about it. The runbook should contain:

  • What this alert means — in plain English, not PromQL
  • Impact — what's affected and who's impacted
  • Immediate actions — step-by-step triage
  • Escalation path — who to contact if you can't resolve it
  • Historical context — links to past incidents caused by this alert

We enforce this with a CI check that parses our alerting rules YAML and fails the build if any alert is missing a runbook_url annotation.

The Results

Month 1: Audit

Cataloged all 147 alerting rules. Found that 68% were threshold-based cause alerts. Tagged each as "keep," "rewrite," or "delete."

Month 2: Rewrite

Rewrote 43 rules using multi-burn-rate approach. Deleted 89 rules entirely. Added runbooks for every remaining alert.

Month 3: Tune

Ran old and new rules in parallel. Compared signal-to-noise ratio. Adjusted burn rates and windows based on real incident data.

Month 4+: Maintain

Weekly review of all fired alerts. Any alert that fires without requiring action gets rewritten or deleted. Alert count stabilized at 10-15/week.

Key Takeaways

  • Alert on symptoms, not causes. Users don't care about your CPU. They care about latency and errors.
  • Use multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting for SLO-based alerts. It's more complex to set up but dramatically reduces noise.
  • Every alert needs a runbook. If you can't write a runbook for an alert, the alert probably shouldn't exist.
  • Inhibition rules prevent alert storms. A node failure shouldn't generate 50 pod alerts.
  • Track your alert quality. Measure signal-to-noise ratio weekly. Any alert that fires without action is a bug in your alerting.
  • Silences are not fixes. Track them, time-limit them, and review them regularly.

"The best alerting system is one where every page means something is broken and the engineer knows exactly what to do about it." — Our SRE team charter
