Platform Engineering vs DevOps – What's the Difference?

The Industry Confusion

Every conference in 2024 had a "Platform Engineering" track. Half the talks were rebranded DevOps presentations. The other half were genuinely different. The confusion is real, and it's costing organizations money — they're hiring "Platform Engineers" to do DevOps work, or expecting DevOps engineers to build internal products without product management skills.

Let me clarify this based on 8 years of doing both.

DevOps: A Culture and Practice

DevOps is a set of practices that combines software development and IT operations. It aims to shorten the development lifecycle and provide continuous delivery. The core principles:

  • Shared responsibility: "You build it, you run it" — developers own their services in production
  • Automation: CI/CD pipelines, infrastructure as code, automated testing
  • Collaboration: Breaking down silos between dev and ops teams
  • Feedback loops: Monitoring, alerting, and incident response inform development

DevOps is not a team. It's not a job title (though it became one). It's a cultural movement that says developers and operations should work together, share tools, and share responsibility.

The "You Build It, You Run It" Problem at Scale

⚠️ The Scaling Problem

When you have 5 teams, "you build it, you run it" works. Every team can learn Kubernetes, Terraform, Prometheus, and CI/CD. When you have 50+ teams, it breaks down. Not every team can — or should — be an expert in infrastructure.

Here's what we observed at our organization with 60 development teams:

  • Each team spent 30-40% of their time on infrastructure instead of product features
  • 15 different ways to deploy to Kubernetes across the org
  • Terraform modules copy-pasted and diverged — security drift everywhere
  • New teams took 3 weeks to get their first service deployed
  • On-call fatigue — developers were paged for infrastructure issues they didn't understand

This is the exact problem Platform Engineering solves.

Platform Engineering: A Product Discipline

Platform Engineering is the discipline of designing and building toolchains and workflows that enable self-service capabilities for software engineering organizations. The key word is product.

Internal Developer Platform (IDP)

An IDP is the product that platform engineers build. It's a layer on top of your infrastructure that provides self-service capabilities to developers. It's not a single tool — it's a combination of tools, APIs, and documentation that work together.

Developer pushes code to Git
IDP triggers CI pipeline (standardized template)
IDP provisions infrastructure (Terraform modules)
IDP deploys to Kubernetes (Helm + ArgoCD)
IDP configures monitoring (Prometheus + Grafana)
Developer sees service running in portal

Golden Paths

Golden paths are opinionated, pre-built workflows that cover 80% of use cases. They're not mandatory — teams can deviate — but they're the recommended way to do things.

Our golden paths include:

  • 🛤️ New service: Backstage template → creates repo, CI pipeline, Helm chart, ArgoCD app, Grafana dashboard in 10 minutes
  • 🛤️ New database: Self-service form → provisions RDS via Crossplane, sets up backups, creates monitoring alerts
  • 🛤️ New environment: PR to infra repo → Terraform provisions VPC, EKS namespace, IAM roles

Team Topologies: Stream-Aligned vs Platform Teams

The Team Topologies framework by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais defines four team types. Platform Engineering maps directly to the "Platform Team" type:

Team Structure

Stream-Aligned Teams (50+ teams): Build and own business features. They consume the platform. They don't manage infrastructure directly.

Platform Team (1 team, 6-8 engineers): Builds and maintains the IDP. Treats stream-aligned teams as customers. Runs user research, collects feedback, prioritizes features.

Enabling Team (2-3 engineers): Helps stream-aligned teams adopt the platform. Writes documentation, runs workshops, provides migration support.

What Platform Engineers Actually Do Day-to-Day

A typical week for our platform team:

Monday

Sprint planning. Review developer feedback from last week's survey. Prioritize: 3 teams requested self-service database provisioning. 1 team needs GPU node support.

Tuesday-Wednesday

Build Crossplane compositions for RDS provisioning. Write Backstage plugin for the self-service form. Update Terraform modules for GPU node groups.

Thursday

Internal demo to 3 pilot teams. Collect feedback. Fix UX issues in the Backstage form. Update documentation.

Friday

Platform reliability work: upgrade ArgoCD, patch Kubernetes, review Prometheus alert rules. Write ADR (Architecture Decision Record) for GPU support design.

💡 The Threshold

If you're hearing the same infrastructure questions from 3+ different teams, it's time to start building a platform. If new teams take more than a week to deploy their first service, you're already late.

Common Mistakes

Mistake 1: Building a Platform Nobody Uses

🚨 Anti-Pattern

We spent 4 months building a custom deployment tool. When we launched it, teams said "we already have scripts that work." We hadn't talked to our users. The tool was technically superior but solved a problem nobody had.

Fix: Start with user research. Interview 5 teams. Find the top 3 pain points. Build for those first. Ship in 2-week increments and get feedback.

Mistake 2: Over-Engineering

Your IDP doesn't need to be Backstage + Crossplane + ArgoCD + Terraform Cloud + custom CLI on day one. Start with a shared Helm chart library and standardized CI templates. That alone saves teams weeks.

Mistake 3: Not Treating Developers as Customers

Platform teams that operate like infrastructure teams — "here's the tool, read the docs" — fail. Successful platform teams run NPS surveys, have office hours, track adoption metrics, and iterate based on feedback.

Our adoption metrics: We track weekly active users of each platform capability. If a golden path has less than 60% adoption after 3 months, we either improve it or kill it. Currently at 84% average adoption across all golden paths.

The Relationship Between DevOps and Platform Engineering

Platform Engineering doesn't replace DevOps. It's an evolution. DevOps principles (automation, shared responsibility, feedback loops) are the foundation. Platform Engineering applies product thinking on top of those principles to scale them across an organization.

"DevOps is the culture. Platform Engineering is the product that scales that culture."

Every platform engineer should understand DevOps deeply. But not every DevOps engineer needs to become a platform engineer. If you enjoy building tools that other engineers use, understanding developer workflows, and thinking about user experience — platform engineering is your path.

✅ Key Takeaway

DevOps says "automate and collaborate." Platform Engineering says "build a product that makes automation and collaboration the default." You need DevOps culture first. Platform Engineering is what you build when that culture needs to scale beyond what individual teams can maintain.
